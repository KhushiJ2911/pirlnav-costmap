Costmap-Conditioned PIRLNav

Does conditioning ObjectNav behavior cloning on a ground-truth geodesic “costmap” instead of RGB change what a policy can learn — across human and shortest-path demonstrations?

TL;DR

Over two phases I built a costmap-conditioned PIRLNav and asked whether an oracle geodesic input beats RGB. Phase 1 (human demos) found a hard ceiling — both RGB and costmap scored 0.0% success on held-out val (vs the paper’s 64%). Phase 2 (shortest-path demos) isolated the costmap’s contribution under a cleaner teacher: costmap+SP beats RGB+SP on every metric (Success 3.2% vs 2.1%, SPL 2.0% vs 1.1%, softSPL 0.225 vs 0.166) at a matched 8M-frame budget.

The takeaway. The costmap interface does help — it changes which teachers are learnable and generalizes better than RGB from shortest-path demos. But absolute performance is capped by a shared ceiling (an ~0.8-epoch budget vs the paper’s ~33, and a limited FOV/depth perception), which no input channel can lift on its own.

Background & the question

PIRLNav trains ObjectNav agents by behavior cloning (BC) on 77k human demonstrations (64.1% success), then RL-finetunes to 70.4%. MASt3R-Nav shows a dense per-pixel cost-to-goal representation (a “WayPixel costmap”) feeding a small controller can beat end-to-end learning.

The internship question: if PIRLNav’s BC policy is conditioned on an oracle (ground-truth) WayPixel-style costmap instead of RGB, does ObjectNav’s difficulty collapse?

Answered in two phases, below — first with the human demonstrations PIRLNav uses, then with shortest-path (SP) demonstrations that sharpen the test.

Method — the costmap sensor

An online sensor computes the ground-truth geodesic distance from every navigable grid point to the nearest goal (one multi-goal shortest-path query per grid point over the navmesh, KD-tree lookup, cached per scene+category — validated against offline GT to MAE 0.06–0.10 m). Each step it back-projects the agent’s depth image onto that field to produce an egocentric costmap the policy sees instead of / alongside RGB.

The 2-channel fix (a key Phase-1 result)

A naïve 1-channel costmap (globally scaled by /30) left the within-frame gradient spanning only ~4% of the input range — the from-scratch encoder ignored it and the policy collapsed to the action-prior floor. The fix, a 2-channel transform:

  • Ch 0 — absolute cost (cost/max): “how far is the goal” → the STOP cue.
  • Ch 1 — per-frame contrast-stretched cost: “which way is downhill” → the steering cue.

This made the input learnable at train time (a 1-scene overfit reaches loss 0.0 / 100% accuracy — the pipeline is certified correct).

Phase 1 · Human demonstrations

Reproducing PIRLNav — and hitting a ceiling

Both arms were trained by BC on human demos at a matched 8M-frame budget (effective batch 16,320 ≈ the paper’s, via gradient accumulation on a single 16 GB GPU). Training fit was healthy and near-identical across arms (action accuracy ~0.74). But on the full 1000-episode HM3D-v2 val split, both scored 0.0% success:

Phase 2 · Shortest-path demonstrations

A sharper test — and the costmap wins

Shortest-path (SP) demos are generated by an oracle that beelines to the goal using privileged geodesic information. PIRLNav shows they’re a terrible teacher for RGB (6.4% vs 64.1%) — the policy can’t see the privileged signal the teacher follows. But the costmap is that signal. So the prediction is an interaction effect: SP demos should stay bad for RGB but become more learnable through the costmap. Both arms were retrained on SP demos at the same 8M-frame budget and evaluated on the same held-out val split.

Why absolute performance is capped

The costmap-vs-RGB result is clear, but every arm sits far below the paper’s 64%. Three entangled causes — none of which the costmap can fix on its own:

1 · Undertraining (budget)

We trained ~0.8 of one epoch; the paper trains BC for ~500M steps on 64 GPUs (~33 epochs). The LR schedule decays to ~0 by 8M steps, forcing learning to stop before even one full pass over the data.

2 · The teacher (Phase 1)

Human demos wander (perfect imitation ≈ SPL 0.47); BC punishes beating the teacher, so a privileged input has little room to help. SP demos remove this — and the costmap advantage appears.

3 · Representation limits

FOV / a 5 m depth window / low resolution. The costmap is back-projected through the same limited depth camera, so it inherits the same ceiling — it can only reveal cost within the visible window, and can’t show a goal beyond the horizon.

Engineering notes

Rebuilding the whole stack on rented GPUs (the lab server was lost) and training the costmap arm to completion surfaced several failure modes, each fixed:

  • 1-ch costmap ignored → the 2-channel contrast-stretch transform.
  • NaN divergence (from-scratch encoder + sharp SP targets) → lr 5e-4 + grad-clip 0.1 + a non-finite-gradient guard.
  • Field-build hangs on pathological navmeshes → offline precompute in hard-terminate()-able subprocesses (Euclidean fallback), loaded from disk.
  • Intermittent CUDA crashes → a self-healing wrapper that auto-resumes from the last checkpoint on any crash and kills+resumes on any hang (final run: 4 attempts, 3 crashes, all recovered automatically to 25k updates).

Limitations & future work

  • Lift the ceiling, then re-run the comparison — wider FOV, deeper depth window, higher-resolution costmap, perhaps a spatial map — so the costmap’s advantage can show in absolute terms.
  • Full-budget training (multiple epochs, stretched LR schedule) to quantify how much of the low absolute success is undertraining.
  • Costmap encoder trains from scratch (RGB rides a pretrained encoder) — an asymmetry that disfavors the costmap, so its win is if anything understated.
  • SP demos never teach exploration — a policy that can only follow a visible cost gradient is stuck when the goal is out of perceptual range.
